Intersectionality and Implicit Bias: A Guide

The purpose of this guide is to discuss topics like intersectionality and implicit bias by examining how identity, power structures, and personal views shape experiences within technology and computing industries. By defining societal frameworks and key terms, this guide provides foundational knowledge to help recognize systemic barriers, question stereotypes, and create true inclusion.

Intersectionality is an analytical tool coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw, and it’s used to examine how multiple identities (like race, gender, class, disability, etc.) intersect and interact. Rather than viewing discrimination through individual categories, intersectionality highlights how these identities are connected and create unique experiences for people.

Implicit Bias refers to automatic associations that affect our thinking or decisions without conscious awareness, and it is usually formed through social conditioning or environmental exposure.

The Implicit Bias Test (IBT) is a tool designed to measure the strength of automatic mental associations between concepts and stereotypes. By tracking split-second reaction times during sorting tasks, the test reveals subconscious preferences that individuals may have.

Reflection on Intersectionality, Implicit Bias, and IBT

Technology has the ability to influence and redefine nearly every part of modern life, from culture and commerce to climate action, healthcare, and communication. In just the past few years, its growth has accelerated dramatically, changing how we find, share, and consume information every day. Because technology is integrated so deeply into our daily lives, it matters who is building it and whose perspectives are shaping it. This is why conversations about intersectionality and true diversity belong at the center of the tech space. When the field welcomes and values a range of identities, it creates space for stronger innovation, less bias, and more inclusive workplaces.

When we look at tech through just one lens (like gender OR race OR disability), we miss the bigger picture of how our full identities intersect with one another. Algorithms and software carry the hidden assumptions and biases of the people who write the code. When teams building these tools all consist of people with the same backgrounds and experiences, we end up with flaws that have real effects. For example, facial recognition that misidentifies people of color, voice assistants that can’t pick up different accents, or apps that ignore accessibility.

On Implicit Bias, we described it as something “sneaky” because it happens when we aren’t even paying attention. None of us wake up in the morning planning to be biased, but because these shortcuts run on autopilot, they influence our daily choices, assumptions, and interactions without us realizing it. You can see this play out everywhere, especially in high-impact fields like tech. A lot of people in tech genuinely care about diversity & inclusion and most want everyone to have a seat at the table. In reality, quiet assumptions can still sneak into who gets hired, who gets promoted, and even how products and software are designed. Wanting to do the right thing isn’t enough – we actually have to check our thinking and hold ourselves accountable for the real-world impact of our actions.

So, taking implicit bias tests can be an eye-opening starting point because it forces you to look at quick, split-second associations you might not know you hard. But it’s super important not to treat your results like a final, unchangeable label on who you are. We believe that since the test mostly measures biases based on response times and reaction speed, it is not about getting a “perfect” score on the test. Recognizing these automatic associations is just the first step in creating an inclusive environment around us.
